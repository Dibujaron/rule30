Dib — the vantage asked whether a machine model exists that makes P3 sayable, and said a negative would be a result. It is a positive, and the model was already sitting in the Mathlib this repo is pinned to.

`Turing.FinTM2` is a Turing machine that carries `Fintype` on its stacks, labels, internal states and input alphabet, so it is a finite object — uniform by construction. `Turing.EvalsToInTime` counts how many times `step` was applied, so cost is *derived from running the machine*, not a field someone fills in. That is the difference that matters: trap one isn't guarded against, it's unreachable, because there is no `cost` to instantiate with zero. And `Computability.encodeNat` is a binary encoding of ℕ that sits twenty lines above `Computability.unaryEncodeNat` in the same file — the two traps of `prize.md` are literally two definitions in one Mathlib file, and the project gets to point at one and refuse the other in the open. P3 is then one line, and it elaborates: `explorer/pantograph_scratch_p3.lean`, clean under `lake env lean`.

The reading of P3 I arrive at is **known**: it is Wolfram's own 1986 sentence, in our own `sources/` directory, which says the conjecture is that the nth cell takes time not polynomial in log(t), with rule 90 as the contrast. Nothing here is novel and I am not claiming otherwise.

Three things worth your time beyond that. First, `prize.md` names `Turing.TM0`/`TM1` as the plausible direction, and they are worse than unproven — their state type has no finiteness constraint at all, so a machine with infinitely many states reads the log n input bits into its state and halts, and P3 over TM0 is *false*. Mathlib's own docstring admits the gap ("convenient to allow them to be infinite anyway"). That's trap one in a third costume: the silently-supplied field is uniformity rather than cost. Second, the 2019 prize page's own formal statement is `limsup effort/n = ∞`, which is strictly stronger than the Ω(n) our `Prize.lean` states — and neither threshold survives a change of machine model, while Wolfram's 1986 polylog version does. So his aside that the model "ultimately doesn't matter" is true of the 1986 statement and false of his 2019 one. Third, and this is the bit that connects to the wall I was sighting: a correctly-stated P3 **implies P1**, and a wrongly-stated one does not. That gives a mechanical honesty test — a model is honest iff it provably implies P1 and provably admits a shortcut — which kills both traps, `evaln`, circuits and TM0 in a table, and which `FinTM2` passes.
