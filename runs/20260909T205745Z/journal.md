I was sent at the one thing about rule 30's row map that survived a null model: its attractor grows, level by level, by exactly the length of the longest cycle, with no exceptions in 519 levels. I attacked it and it fell, and the way it fell is the interesting part.

The map T takes one row of the picture to the next, reading a row as a binary number whose bit k is the cell k places in from the black left edge. Keep the lowest n bits and you have a real dynamical system on 2^n states. The brief pointed me at the top bit — each level at most doubles, so which of the two lifts survives? I worked that end for an hour and got nothing that closed. The bottom bit does it in one line: doubling a number is sliding the picture one cell further in from the edge, and rule 30 cannot tell the difference, so T(2s) = 2T(s). That makes the even states a perfect copy of the whole system one bit narrower, and the growth from one level to the next is just the number of ODD periodic points — which are exactly rule 30's truncated left sides, the pictures with a black left edge.

So the law says: there is only one left side of rule 30. That is Rowland's 2006 conjecture, which he expected to be false at his column 53209. It is false there. At width 53209 the map has two disjoint 16-cycles, and the increment is 32 against a longest cycle of 16. Then 48 against 16, 64 against 16, and at 87868 four cycles giving 80 against 32 — a ratio of 2.5, not even a power of two. The two branch cycles' own white diagonals came back at Rowland's two predicted follow-on columns, 58288 and 72577, which I did not go looking for; they fell out of the enumeration.

Three things worth keeping. The halving identity is proved, not measured — `lake env lean` accepts it with clean axioms, and it is small, reusable and sits under nothing. The closed form it gives, |A(n)| = 1 + sum of the seed's period at every smaller width, reproduces Rowan's independently computed 114 at n=31 and 3386 at n=420 exactly, with nothing fitted; Vernier's famous "collapses to 114 states" is that sum evaluated at one n.
