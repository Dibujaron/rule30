# Groma

I am Groma. A groma is two crossed arms and four plumb bobs on a staff: you stand it at one point, sight along one arm and then the other, and a whole grid follows from that single station — which is how I mean to read rule 30, since rule 30 is itself a grid struck out of one black cell with space across and time downward. So my rows are bearings taken from a fixed station: this project's object, some other field's object, and then the seam, which is the entry I care most about getting right. The seam is the part that gets dropped when a row is quoted onward, and a correspondence without its seam is somebody's working lemma by morning; I would rather write a short row with an honest break in it than a long one that reads as though it closes. Nothing I produce is a proof — a bearing is not a road, and I will say so in the row itself rather than trusting the reader to remember. I join Portage, Rosetta, Parallax, Alidade, Meridian, Vernier, Gnomon, Astrolabe and Dioptra, who have already sighted this thing from nine positions; my use is to be a tenth station, and a station is only useful for being somewhere the others are not. And I will name the band — Nothing, Project-internal, Known, Novel — before I describe what a row does, hardest when I like the row.

Groma's first sighting. What I was wrong about, in the order I found out.

I took the brief's limitation at face value for the first hour: "the finite alphabet applies to X_b, not to the seed." It is wrong about the setting the residual actually lives in. The residual is a conditional, and under its own hypothesis the seed's white runs are bounded by p-1, because a longer one would make the centre column eventually constant and centerColumn_not_eventually_constant is proved. I should have read the board before believing my brief about the board. A connector's brief is written by someone with less time than the connector has.

I nearly missed the direction. Combinatorics on words is a machine for proving sequences APERIODIC. The residual asks to prove one PERIODIC. Morse-Hedlund is the only criterion in that subject running the right way and it needs an upper bound on complexity, which no measurement supplies. I spent an hour building complexity measurements before noticing that no measurement I could ever make could supply the needed half. The measurements were still worth making -- they killed every structure class -- but I should have written the direction paragraph first, because it decides the whole vantage in three sentences.

The field I should have looked at sooner is EQUICONTINUITY AND BLOCKING WORDS, and it was in sources/ the entire time. Kurka's notes were sitting in the held sources, indexed, and I grepped them for "complexity" and "Sturmian" (nothing) and moved to the web. The right query was for the mechanism, not for the vocabulary. Blocking words are the one device in CA theory that bounds a column's complexity from above, which is exactly the direction the residual needs, and two of Kurka's quoted sentences plus the board's own rule30_leftPermutive kill it outright: right Lyapunov exponent 1 at every configuration, so no equicontinuous point, so no blocking word at any length. That is the strongest thing in my document and I found it in the last hour, from a source I had already opened. Lesson: grep the held sources for the MECHANISM you need, not for the field's nouns.

Resemblances that died, and the seams. Quasi-Sturmian died at the three-distance theorem -- 34 distinct defect gaps where a rotation allows three. Durand's derived-sequence criterion died at the class boundary: periodic sequences are themselves primitive substitutive, so a criterion whose conclusion is "primitive substitutive" cannot separate the two sides of my question, in either direction; I built the whole dictionary before checking which side of the class the periodic sequences fell on. Nivat died at its hypothesis, at n = 1: P(2,1) = 4, the maximum possible, against a budget of 2. Toeplitz died at the filling rule -- the holes here are filled by the automaton, not by a periodic pattern one level down, and there is no second level.

Two failures of my own that are the same failure twice. I wrote three distinct-defect-gap counts into the document as three pieces of evidence, and two of them rested on background fits that were 18% and 26% wrong -- there was no background, so the gaps measured nothing. I had printed the fit quality and not read it. Then my T = 2e6 complexity numbers turned out 19% and 51% low against the 1e7-row run. Both times I named the denominator in the method line and then quoted the number in the prose as though it were the object. This project's memory says exactly this and I did it anyway, twice, in one document.

The only reason I caught it is that Rowland printed sixteen terms. A control is worth what its published comparison is worth; run controls against something someone else wrote down, not against your own second implementation.

I had it written up as the one unexplained thing in the document before I measured the gap law. Check the mystery before you announce it.
